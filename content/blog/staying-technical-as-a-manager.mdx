There is a version of engineering management where you stop writing code, and the
titles get more senior while your hands get further from the work. There is another
version where you keep taking tickets, and you become the reason nothing ships when
you are on leave. I have watched both go badly, and I have been closer to the second
than I would like.

When I managed a team at BYJU'S, I also set up and ran our AWS infrastructure —
instances, storage, access control, networking. On paper that is a strange thing for
an engineering manager to be doing. I think it was the right call, but not for the
reason I would have given at the time.

## The case against picking up the work

The objections are real and I do not want to wave them away:

- **You become a bottleneck.** Work waits on you specifically, which is the opposite
  of what a manager is for.
- **You lose the plot.** Context-switching between a debugging session and a
  one-on-one about someone's career serves neither.
- **You take the interesting work.** If the manager absorbs every architectural
  problem, the senior engineers do not get the reps they need to grow into the next
  level. This one is the most damaging and the easiest to do without noticing.

## The case for staying close enough to judge

The counter-argument is not "managers should code". It is that a manager who cannot
evaluate technical work is managing on vibes, and the people doing the work can tell
within about two weeks.

That manifests in specific, expensive ways. You approve an estimate you cannot
interrogate. You accept a design because it is described confidently. You cannot tell
the difference between an engineer who is stuck and an engineer who is being
thorough. You end up relying on whoever speaks most fluently, which is a selection
pressure you did not intend to apply.

Infrastructure was where I chose to stay close, because the decisions there are
mostly *judgement* decisions rather than *typing* decisions. How do you segment the
network? Who can assume which role? What is the blast radius when a key leaks? Those
are the questions that decide whether an incident is a bad afternoon or a bad quarter,
and they are exactly the questions a manager should be able to argue about.

## The split that worked

The line I landed on:

- **Stay in the decisions that underwrite your judgement.** Architecture, cost,
  security posture, the shape of a data model, the risk of a dependency. You need
  enough fluency to disagree with a confident engineer, and that is a different bar
  from being the one who implements.
- **Hand off the execution, deliberately.** If you are the only one who can do a
  thing, that is a bus-factor problem with your name on it, and it is yours to fix.
- **Never take the interesting problem by default.** Choose what you keep, say why,
  and let someone else take the rest on purpose rather than by attrition.

<Callout>
  The question is not "should a manager write code". It is "which decisions must I be
  able to argue about, and am I still fluent enough in those to argue about them
  honestly".
</Callout>

## One-on-ones are the unit of management

The other half of this is that the time you buy by not being in the code has to go
somewhere useful. For me that is one-on-ones, which are the only recurring mechanism
that reliably surfaces a problem before it becomes a resignation.

The things that made them work were unglamorous: keeping them at a fixed time so they
are not a signal, letting the other person set the agenda, writing down what was
agreed and following up on it, and not turning them into status updates. A one-on-one
where the manager does most of the talking is a stand-up with worse acoustics.

## OKRs, carefully

I have also seen OKRs do real damage — a set of key results invented in a workshop and
then ignored until the quarter ends, or worse, pursued after they stopped mattering.
The version that helps is smaller: a few outcomes that the team genuinely believes in,
revisited often enough that changing one is not an admission of failure.

## The compounding part

The thread running through my career that I would keep, if I could keep only one
thing, is mentoring — from the associates I worked with early on, to the engineers I
grew as a lead, to the people I manage now. It is slow, it does not show up in a
sprint, and it is the only thing I have done that makes the team better after I leave
it. Everything else is a delivery. That is a capability.
