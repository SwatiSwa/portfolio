On a lending platform, the interesting engineering is never the screen the customer
sees. The checkout is a button. Everything hard is underneath it, and most of it is
somebody else's API behaving in a way nobody documented.

Over a couple of years I integrated payment and lending partners including Paytm,
PayU, Bajaj, Pine Labs, Kotak, ICICI, Zest, Avanse, IIFL and RBL, alongside PAN India
APIs, credit bureau checks through TransUnion CIBIL, and penny drop for validating
customer bank accounts. Ten integrations is enough to see the pattern, and the
pattern is that there is no pattern.

## Every gateway is a different shape

The mistake I made early was looking for the common denominator. There isn't one,
and hunting for it produces an abstraction with a hole in the middle where each
gateway's real behaviour should be.

<Callout>
  Ten payment partners differ on authentication, on whether the outcome arrives by
  callback or by polling, on how they name a refund, on whether amounts are paise or
  rupees or a string, on how long a session stays valid, and on what "failed" means.
  Design for that difference rather than trying to dissolve it.
</Callout>

| What differs | Why it hurts |
| --- | --- |
| Status vocabulary | One gateway's `PENDING` is another's "we never received it". Mapping these onto a shared enum loses the distinction you need at 2am. |
| Completion signal | Callback, redirect, or poll. Each needs a different recovery path when the customer closes the tab. |
| Amount representation | Paise as integer, rupees as decimal, rupees as string. Exactly one of these rounds badly. |
| Error taxonomy | An insufficient-funds failure is a retry-later problem. An invalid-account failure is a support-call problem. Both arrive as a numeric code that means something different at every partner. |

## Keep their words out of your domain

The rule that saved the most pain was the simplest one: no partner's status string,
error code or identifier should ever reach our own domain model. Each partner gets an
adapter that translates in both directions, and the core only ever sees our
vocabulary.

```ts
// The core knows about Payment, not about any particular gateway.
interface PaymentGateway {
  authorize(request: PaymentRequest): Promise<AuthResult>
  capture(id: GatewayRef): Promise<CaptureResult>
  refund(id: GatewayRef, amount: Money): Promise<RefundResult>
  // Their transport, their auth, their codes — all in here, none of it out there.
}
```

It is more code than calling the SDK directly, and it is the only reason supporting
the tenth partner was a week's work rather than a quarter's.

## The parts nobody puts on the roadmap

**Idempotency.** A customer double-taps a payment button, or a callback is delivered
twice, or a retry succeeds after a timeout that was not actually a failure. Without an
idempotency key of your own, each of these is a duplicate payment.

**Reconciliation.** Somebody must compare what your database believes happened with
what the partner's settlement file says happened, and it must run even on the days
nothing appears to have gone wrong. Reconciliation written after the first incident
is written in a hurry.

**Sandboxes that lie.** Test environments accept amounts that production rejects,
approve accounts that do not exist, and time out differently. A flow that passes in
sandbox is evidence about your code, not about their system.

## Penny drop: clever, and sharp

Penny drop validates a bank account by transferring one rupee into it and reading the
account holder's name back from the response. It is a genuinely clever use of the
payment rails as an identity check, and it is the reason you can onboard a borrower
without a cancelled cheque.

It also has edges that are easy to miss. The name that comes back is whatever the bank
holds, which is frequently not the name the customer typed — initials, honorifics,
joint accounts and transliteration between scripts all produce mismatches that are
not fraud. It costs money per attempt, it is not instant, and the account can be
valid. The design decision is what you do with a partial match, and that is a
business decision you should push upward rather than settle in the code.

## Credit checks are not free

A CIBIL pull through TransUnion is billable, rate-limited and, in the customer's eyes,
a thing you did to them. Running one on every form submission is an expensive habit.
The engineering work here is mostly about not doing things: caching a recent report,
checking eligibility rules before spending a bureau call, and making sure a retry
after a timeout does not silently become a second pull.

## What I would do first, next time

Model payments as your own domain with adapters at the edges, build the idempotency
key before the first integration rather than after the first duplicate, and treat the
reconciliation report as a feature with a place on the roadmap. None of it is
glamorous. All of it is what stands between a working demo and a system that a finance
team is willing to trust.
